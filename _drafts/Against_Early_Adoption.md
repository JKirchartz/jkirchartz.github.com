I used to be an Early Adopter; somebody who chased the hot new thing and only
wanted the newest and "best" things. I've since learned that I was wrong.<!--more-->

I wasn't getting the best; just the newest; the one with the shiniest
packaging, the most glitz and glamour. But it was never much better than the
thing I had before, it just had that "new car smell" that we somehow find so
alluring. I was buying the sizzle instead of the steak, without realizing
Salsbury can sizzle just as loud as any T-Bone. That "New Car Smell" is part of
the experience; and that's what I was buying, an experience, the same
experience over and over. The Hot New Thing was, maybe, marginally, 5-10%
better than the old thing, but still did the same thing just in a different
way.

You can only be burned so many times before you learn your lesson. So, Lesson Learned.

For example, After Flash and Actionscript went the way of the Dodo, [I was an
early adopter of a JS Framework called Protaculous, but jQuery won
out](2009/06/opinion-protaculous-to-jquery.html), and [Vanilla JS won in the
end](http://vanilla-js.com/), but now there's [lots of different ways to write
Javascipt](2021/07/in_defense_of_javascript.html) that aren't so vanilla, it
seems Typescript is currently in the lead, at least partially due to it being a
Microsoft product that works well with Facebook's React framework.

If a new technique is legitimately better I will adopt it, but it needs to
prove itself first.  Ultimately, the most legitimate reason to be an early
adopter is Eating your own Dogfood, or Drinking your own Champagne, whicher you
prefer to call it. Innovating automatically makes you the earliest adopter, and
this is where you can really shine. But you should be as wary of that as you
are of your Project Manager's Homebrew Kombucha that's allegedly "only Slightly
Alcoholic" or "Grandma's Kimchi Recipe" that's "only a little spicy"; when
you're cooking the dish, you can taste it as often as you need to ensure
Quality.

There's an ancient hacker motto "Free as in Speech, Free as in Beer, Free as-in
Puppy," meaning that you can do whatever you want with the software, there's no
up-front cost, but while you get it for nothing, you're required to nurture and
feed it for years to come; But the Puppies are being replaced by Robot Dogs,
and these machines cannot tell the difference between flesh and steel and have
no requirement to make a distinction. This motto has a new addendum: ["Free
as-in Tribbles"](https://nesbitt.io/2026/05/07/free-as-in-tribbles.html); where
&mdash;like the Star Trek creatures&mdash; there's simply too big of a hidden
costs, too many hidden risks, too many attacks on the every part of the system,
where the only solution seems to be to jetisson it off into space.

What is really is is a it question of Trust. Some vendor may offer what you
need at a reasonable price; but who can you trust to work for your benefit? Is
that free toolchain ready for prime-time? Is it going to [stay
free?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musk_v._Altman) What are the [hidden
costs?](https://techstartups.com/2026/05/28/company-accidentally-spent-500-million-on-claude-ai-in-one-month-after-forgetting-usage-limits/)
When things go off the rails, things can become quite costly.[^costly] The
threat of
[Enshittification](https://pluralistic.net/2023/01/21/potemkin-ai/#hey-guys) is
looming over all of us. It's getting harder and harder to know if the person
you're trusting online is even a
[person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dead_Internet_theory). All this can't be
good for your mind; yet we soldier on.

I'd like to think of myself as well-positioned in these tying times; and maybe
I might, once again, be being too Cynical, but I prefer to call it
"Cosmopolitan Realism." As a Citizen of the World, I like to try to see it for
what it really is, sometimes when you get burned you need to fight fire with
fire, but it's more common sense to fight fire with water[^firefighting]. Or, get
away and let the heat die down. You see more when you take a step back, if you
can, and consider a problem from the outside of it; look at it from every
angle; and then get back into the fray to figure it out. And like Firefighters,
it makes it a lot easier to work together and to actually know what you're
doing. In software engineering it's not always life and death, [although
sometimes it is](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Therac-25); in firefighting it's
almost always that serious.

Physical Engineering knowledge is learned through blood, sweat, and tears
&mdash; not as metaphor, but as a harsh reality.  I learned more about that
from one of the best books I've ever read, "To Engineer is Human: The Role of
Failure in Successful Design" by Henry Petrovski where he says:

> “Engineers ... are not superhuman. They make mistakes in their assumptions,
> in their calculations, in their conclusions. That they make mistakes is
> forgivable; that they catch them is imperative. Thus it is the essence of
> modern engineering not only to be able to check one's own work but also to
> have one's work checked and to be able to check the work of others.”

Civil Engineering and Arcitectural projects failure costs lives, let alone
millions of dollars in lawsuits. In software the stakes aren't usually that
high, even though there's still legal action, but we're still serving the same
Human cause. For the same Human goals. The Economy is humans moving money
around, Work is humans moving resources around; but the simulation is just a
simulation until enacted in the world, now we've automated the simulation
making real effects on the Internet.

I adopted AI very early on, from 2015-2021 I participated in
[NaNoGenMo](https://jkirchartz.com/NaNoGenMo/), writing software to generate a
50,000 word novella; there were a lot of novel approaches, lots of different
creative methods and techniques to take a corpus and generate something new
from it. Even with Neural Nets, the results were never that good; the best
results in generative writing are curated by humans; even now, 10 years later,
the LLMs still aren't good &mdash;with billions of more times processing power
than I ever had &mdash; and a human in the loop is preferred to letting the
machines run rampant. What I learned from NaNoGenMo is that if you wanted an AI
assistant to perfectly replicate you, they'd need to have lived your life and
experienced it in ways that would invade your privacy, ways you'd never allow
some faceless corporate entity to keep on permanent record; but they collect
the data we give them and use it for marketing and advertising and politics,
and whatever else they please; because it's still a regulatory wild-wild west,
and that's what's driving the funding and payouts and successes. The Unknown is
more exciting than the Boring Truth.

AI may fool some of the people some of the time, but it can't fool all the
people all of the time. The Hype Wave is dying down, and the Hardware isn't
materializing to fulfill the needs of the software fast enough, and the
software isn't delivering on promises because it's too busy [hallicinating
about weird things it read on the
internet.](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2026/04/openai-codex-system-prompt-includes-explicit-directive-to-never-talk-about-goblins/)
The hard part of NaNoGenMo, to me, seemed to be to clean up the data into a
format that the rest of the software could actually use instead of mutilate it,
but even with big software companies having the LLMs and hundreds of developers
working around the clock to prompt and hopefully further train these LLMS, a
lot of money has been spent but not a lot of results seem to have taken off.
Personally, I don't want to do Q/A testing and help train the AIs that require
failing harder and faster than any human ever possibly could just for a chance
to find a potential jackpot; the only winners are the ones running the
casino.

[The only humanity the AI has is what us Humans bring with
us.](https://posts.decontextualize.com/language-models-poetry/) It will always
be [limited by inherent paradoxes in the language of
Mathmatics](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/mathematical-paradox-demonstrates-the-limits-of-ai),
before we even get to the language of Logic or the language of
[rhetoricians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gorgias_\(dialogue\)) and
[sophists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophist_\(dialogue\)), people [lying on
the internet is a security risk for
AI](https://www.sentinelone.com/cybersecurity-101/cybersecurity/data-poisoning/),
and AI Brokers will pass the liability of those lies onto their clients. The Big
Players like Amazon, Google, and Microsoft can eat the costs, but can you?
